Field Notes on Slow Bread

Wild yeast, patient dough, and the bakers keeping a slower craft alive in a fast-proof world.

By Mara Okafor · Photographs by Teo Lindqvist · 18 min read
In this piece
  1. The starter
  2. Time as an ingredient
  3. The numbers
  4. The oven

Before sunrise in a converted tannery outside Asheville, the only sound is the hum of a walk-in cooler and the soft slap of dough meeting wood. Joan Pereira has been folding the same starter for nineteen years. She calls it Augustine, and she will tell you, unprompted, that it is older than her marriage.

Slow bread is less a recipe than a schedule. Flour, water, and salt do most of the talking; the baker's real job is deciding when to stay out of the way. A long, cold proof lets enzymes break starches into sugars the yeast can work with, and lets bacteria build the acids that give a loaf its depth.

The starter

Every starter is a small ecosystem. Wild yeasts and lactic acid bacteria arrive on the flour and in the air, and the ones that thrive at a bakery's particular temperature and feeding rhythm become its signature. Move a starter across the country and, within weeks, it starts to taste of its new kitchen.

Time as an ingredient

Commercial bread can go from bag to shelf in under three hours. Pereira's takes three days. The difference shows up in flavor, in keeping quality, and, some bakers argue, in how easily the bread is digested.

“You can't rush the dough. You can only schedule your life around it.”

That schedule is unforgiving. A warm afternoon can over-proof a whole batch; a cold snap can leave it sluggish. The bakers who last are the ones who learn to read the dough rather than the clock.

The oven

The final hour belongs to fire. Loaves go in at 250 °C onto stone that has soaked up heat overnight, and steam keeps the crust supple long enough for the bread to bloom. When the crackle starts as the loaves cool, Pereira says, that is the bread talking back.

The people

Pereira employs six bakers, and none of them trained in a culinary school. Two came from carpentry, one from nursing, one from a decade of night shifts at a distribution center. She looks for patience and steady hands, and teaches the rest at the bench, one fold at a time.

The work is physical and the hours are strange. Shifts start at three in the morning and end before most offices open. What keeps people, she says, is the feedback: by the end of every shift there is something real on the racks, and by lunch it is gone.

“Bread is the only job I've had where you can see the whole thing finished before noon.”

The bakery sells out most days by early afternoon. Pereira has resisted every suggestion to scale up, open a second shop, or sell to grocery chains. Doubling the output, she argues, would mean doubling the speed, and speed is the one thing the dough will not forgive.

By seven the first customers are at the door. Most of them have no idea the loaf they are buying was started on Tuesday.
